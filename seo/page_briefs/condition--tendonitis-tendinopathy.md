## Purpose

Own the general tendon-pain term, act as the conceptual parent for every tendon presentation on the site, and make the load-capacity argument once, properly, so the body-part pages can reference rather than repeat it.

## Reader

Someone whose Achilles, patellar tendon, gluteal tendon, hamstring origin or shoulder has been sore for weeks to months, usually with a name attached — a doctor, a coach or Google has already said "tendinitis". Classic pattern: sore and stiff at the start of activity, warms up during, hurts more the next morning.

They've usually been told to rest and stretch it. It's improved and relapsed at least once. They're now suspicious that nobody has a real plan.

Five-second need: *this is a known thing with a known approach, and rest wasn't it.*

## Search Intent

Commercial. `tendonitis treatment` (1,000) with `tendinopathy treatment` (260) behind it — a spelling and terminology split where the lay term carries most of the volume. Competition is very low (≤0.12).

Note the terminology trap: the reader searches "tendonitis", the correct term is usually "tendinopathy", and the page has to hold both without a pedantic correction. Use the reader's word first, introduce the accurate one as useful information rather than as a rebuke.

### 3. Tendonitis or tendinopathy?
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** Answer the terminology question early and briefly, then move on. It's the reason people land here and it should take 100 words, not 400.
**Key points:**
- "-itis" means inflammation; genuine inflammatory tendonitis is mostly a short, early-stage phenomenon
- Most persistent tendon pain is a tendinopathy — a change in tendon structure and load tolerance
- Why the distinction matters: it's the difference between "settle it down" and "build it up"
- Both terms are used, including by clinicians, and the reader hasn't got it wrong

### 4. How tendon pain behaves
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** The recognition block. Tendinopathy has an unusually specific and consistent symptom signature — describe it accurately and the reader will feel diagnosed.
**Key points:**
- Warm-up phenomenon: worst at the start, eases as you go, then worse hours later or the next morning
- Localised, point-specific pain rather than a diffuse ache
- Load-dependent, and dose-dependent — a bigger session yesterday means a worse morning today
- Stiffness after sitting still
- Not usually swollen or hot, which is exactly why the inflammation model doesn't fit
- Where we see it most: Achilles, patellar tendon (jumper's knee), gluteal tendon (lateral hip), hamstring origin (sit-bone pain), rotator cuff, elbow

### 5. Why it happened
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** The load-capacity argument, stated as the site's canonical version. Other pages link here rather than restating it.
**Key points:**
- Tendons adapt to load, but slowly — slower than muscle and much slower than motivation
- Almost every case follows a change: volume, intensity, surface, footwear, a new program, a return after time off, or a life change that reduced capacity (sleep, stress, illness, a period off)
- Capacity can also fall while load stays the same, which is why "I didn't change anything" is a common and legitimate report
- Contributing factors handled cautiously: age, some medications, metabolic health, hormonal change (particularly relevant for lateral hip and Achilles presentations around menopause). Mention as recognised associations, never as blame or diagnosis.

### 6. Why rest and stretching make it worse
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** The conversion argument. State it firmly and then qualify it honestly.
**Key points:**
- Rest lowers pain and lowers capacity at the same time — you come back to the same load with less tolerance
- Stretching compresses some tendons at their insertion, which can genuinely aggravate them (Achilles insertion, gluteal tendon, hamstring origin are the common examples)
- The honest qualifier: reducing the aggravating load temporarily is often part of the plan. The distinction is between managed offloading and doing nothing.
- Turn: tendons don't need rest, they need the right dose

### 7. How we treat it
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** Demonstrate a real, staged approach without publishing a program.
**Key points:**
- Work out the load history first — what changed in the weeks before it started
- Settle the irritability: isometrics are commonly used for pain in the early phase
- Progressive loading, heavy and slow, progressed on how the tendon responds over 24 hours rather than how it feels during
- Then energy-storage and springy loading for anyone returning to running or jumping
- Address the chain around it — calf capacity for Achilles, hip and glute strength for patellar and lateral hip
- Hands-on treatment as an adjunct for symptom relief, honestly positioned as not the mechanism of change
- **Timeframe honesty: three to six months is a realistic frame for a longstanding tendinopathy.** Say it. Underselling the timeline is why people quit.
