## Purpose

Serve a high-volume, high-anxiety, high-clinical-risk search safely: give genuinely useful red-flag guidance, explain what structured concussion management involves, and convert the persistent-symptoms searcher — without ever implying Alpha diagnoses concussion or clears anyone to play.

## Reader

Three arrivals, and the first one is the reason this page carries the strictest constraints on the site.

1. **Acute, possibly right now.** Someone was knocked in a game this morning. This may be the parent, the partner, or the team manager searching on a phone at the ground. **They may be in a genuine medical emergency.** Everything they need must be above the fold and unmissable.
2. **Days in, symptoms not settling.** Headache, foggy, light-sensitive, sleep is off, can't concentrate at work. Worried it isn't going away.
3. **Weeks to months in.** Persistent symptoms. Has been told to sit in a dark room and wait. This is the reader Alpha can genuinely help most and the page's commercial centre.

Five-second need for reader 1: *when do I go to hospital.* Serve that before anything else.

## Search Intent

Commercial term (`concussion treatment` 3,600, comp 0.06) sitting on top of an overwhelmingly informational and anxious search population. Low competition reflects the fact that few clinics write this page well, mostly because doing so safely is hard.

The realistic commercial capture is reader 3 — persistent symptoms, sub-acute and chronic management. Readers 1 and 2 are served as a duty of care and as a trust investment, not as conversions. Write the page in that order of priority anyway.

### 2. Emergency guidance — above the fold
**Component:** dedicated alert block (design system must support a high-contrast, non-dismissible callout)
**Direction:** **This block comes before the hero content, not after it.** No marketing copy above it. It is the single most important element on the page and it must be visible without scrolling on mobile.
**Key points:**
- **Call 000 or go to the nearest emergency department now if any of the following are present after a head knock:** loss of consciousness, seizure or convulsion, repeated vomiting, worsening or severe headache, increasing confusion or agitation, unusual drowsiness or difficulty waking, weakness, numbness or tingling, slurred speech, double or blurred vision, neck pain or tenderness, unequal pupils, or any deterioration.

- Any suspected concussion should be assessed by a medical doctor. State it plainly.
- Anyone on blood thinners, with a bleeding disorder, or who has had a previous concussion within the last few weeks warrants a lower threshold for emergency assessment.

### 4. What a concussion actually is
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** Educate carefully and accurately. This is a brain injury and should be described as one, without catastrophising.
**Key points:**
- A mild traumatic brain injury — a functional disturbance rather than a structural one, which is why standard scans usually look normal. Explaining that pre-empts the "but my CT was clear so why do I feel like this" confusion, which is one of the most common and distressing experiences for reader 3.
- You do not have to be knocked out. Most concussions involve no loss of consciousness.
- A blow to the body, not just the head, can cause it.
- Symptom clusters: physical (headache, nausea, dizziness, light and noise sensitivity), cognitive (fog, slowed thinking, memory), emotional (irritability, low mood, anxiety), sleep (too much, too little, unrefreshing).
- Symptoms can appear or worsen over the first 24–48 hours — which is why "he seemed fine after" doesn't rule it out.

### 5. The first few days
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** Practical guidance for reader 2, aligned with current consensus practice. Accuracy matters more than voice here.
**Key points:**
- Relative rest for the first 24–48 hours — reduced physical and cognitive load, not a dark room for a week. Be clear the prolonged-dark-room advice is outdated.
- After the initial period, a gradual return to light activity below the symptom threshold is what current guidance supports.
- Avoid alcohol, avoid a second knock, be cautious with driving, be careful with screens if they aggravate symptoms.
- Someone should keep an eye on the person for the first 24 hours, with the red flags from section 2 restated as a short recap.
- No return to contact or collision activity while symptomatic, and not without medical clearance.

### 6. When symptoms don't settle
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** Reader 3's section, and the page's commercial and emotional centre. Validate hard — this population is routinely dismissed and often ends up doubting themselves.
**Key points:**
- Most people settle within a couple of weeks; a meaningful minority do not, and that is a recognised outcome, not a personal failing
- What persistent symptoms actually look like in a life: can't get through a work day, can't tolerate the supermarket, headaches every afternoon, snapping at your kids, given up training
- Common drivers that respond to targeted rehab — cervical spine contribution (the neck almost always takes the same force as the head), vestibular and balance dysfunction, oculomotor and visual issues, autonomic/exercise intolerance, and the deconditioning and mood effects of extended rest
- The turn: "rest and wait" is not a plan for someone at week six, and there are structured approaches that can be assessed
- This is where the neck link earns its place → /conditions/neck-pain/ and /conditions/headaches/

### 7. How we work with concussion
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** Describe scope honestly and state the boundary explicitly.
**Key points:**
- **State the boundary on the page:** we don't diagnose concussion and we don't clear anyone to return to play — that sits with your doctor. What we do is the rehab in between.
- Assessment: symptom profile, cervical spine, balance and vestibular screening, oculomotor screening, exertion tolerance
- Treatment where indicated: cervical spine treatment and rehab, vestibular and oculomotor exercises, graded sub-symptom-threshold exercise, sleep and load management
- Graded return-to-activity progression, communicated back to the GP or sports doctor
- Referral out when it's warranted — say we'll tell you if you need a neurologist, sports physician, psychologist or optometrist

### 8. Return to learn, work and sport
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** High-search-value, high-risk. Describe the framework generically; do not present Alpha as the decision-maker at any step.
**Key points:**
- Return-to-learn and return-to-work usually precede return-to-sport — a genuinely useful point most pages skip
- The staged return-to-sport framework in general terms: symptom-limited activity → light aerobic → sport-specific → non-contact drills → full contact after medical clearance → return to play
- Each stage typically at least 24 hours; back a stage if symptoms return
- **The contact stage requires medical clearance. State this every time the framework is mentioned.**
- Note that sporting bodies (AFL, community sport, school) have their own mandatory protocols and timeframes, and those override anything on this page. For children and adolescents the protocols are more conservative.

## AHPRA Compliance Notes

**Highest clinical-risk page on the site. Nothing ships without a compliance review by a registered practitioner.**

- The emergency block is a patient-safety requirement, not a design choice. It must not be moved below the fold, made dismissible, or softened for aesthetics at any viewport.
- **Alpha does not diagnose concussion and does not clear return to play.** This must be stated on the page, and no section may contradict it.
- No claim that any treatment speeds recovery, prevents post-concussion syndrome, or reduces risk of long-term effects.
- Do not mention CTE or long-term degenerative outcomes. It cannot be discussed responsibly at this length and would be fear-based marketing.
- Paediatric concussion is more conservative — either state that clearly or state that this page addresses adults and children should follow their sporting body's and doctor's protocol.
- All guidance should be consistent with current international consensus. **Date-stamp the page and set a scheduled review**; concussion guidance changes and stale advice here is a real liability.
- No testimonials on this page.
