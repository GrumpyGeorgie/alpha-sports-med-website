## Purpose

Sell a specific, bookable, paid assessment — with enough concrete detail (what happens, how long, what it costs, what you leave with) that a runner can decide on the page — and anchor the running cluster that ties the conditions, community and blog content together.

## Reader

Two distinct audiences, and **the page currently fails the second one on most competitor sites.**

**1. The injured runner (primary).** Shin splints, ITB, knee pain, Achilles, plantar fascia, a niggle that comes back at the same distance every time. She has already: rested it, changed shoes, watched YouTube, and possibly seen someone who told her to stop running. **That last instruction is the emotional centre of the page** — it is the playbook's single most-cited piece of bad advice, and this reader has usually received it. She wants someone to find out *why*, not to be told to stop.

**2. The runner who isn't injured (secondary — the playbook's proactive audience).** Training for a first half or a marathon, or coming back after time off, and wants to know whether anything in how they run is going to cost them at week 14 of the block. **This audience is under-served and worth explicit inclusion** — most gait analysis pages assume injury, so a runner without pain reads three paragraphs and leaves. Give them their own named section.

Both are high health literacy, will happily read detail, and are strongly influenced by whether the page sounds like it was written by someone who runs.

**First five seconds must deliver:** this is a real assessment with real methodology, here's what happens in it, here's what it costs, and nobody is going to just tell you to stop running.

## Search Intent

Commercial and specific. `running gait analysis` (170) and `running assessment melbourne` (40) are low-volume but very high intent — this searcher knows what she wants and is choosing a provider.

The volume is small; the value is not. This page is:
- A **conversion page** with a defined paid product
- The **hub of the running cluster** (`/conditions/running-injuries/` ↔ here ↔ `/runwest-run-club/` ↔ running blog posts)
- A **differentiation asset** — it is concrete proof of playbook pillar 3, "show the depth publicly"
- Present in the **primary navigation**, which tells you how the client values it

**The competitive reality to write against:** most local "gait analysis" is a running-shoe retailer putting someone on a treadmill for ninety seconds to sell them a shoe. That is what the reader expects and what she is comparing against. Do not attack it — just be conspicuously more thorough, and let the contrast do the work.

## Sections

### 1. Hero
**Component:** `hero-banner`
**Direction:** Real footage or photography of an actual assessment at an Alpha clinic. Treadmill, cameras, a practitioner. **Stock imagery of a silhouetted runner at sunset will lose this reader instantly** — she has seen a thousand of them and none of them told her anything.
**Key points:**
- H1 with "running assessment" and gait
- Subhead: a full assessment of how you actually run — what's driving the injury, or what's likely to
- Trust strip: video gait analysis · strength and movement testing · training load review · a written plan you keep
- **Duration and price in or immediately under the hero.** Cost transparency is a stated requirement and a genuine differentiator; burying it costs bookings.
- CTA: Book online · (03) 9962 5539

### 2. "Just stop running for six weeks"
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** ~150 words. Straight from the playbook. This is the page's emotional hook and Alpha's clearest positioning statement anywhere on the site.
**Key points:**
- Name the experience: the injury that returns at the same point every block; the advice to rest; the six weeks off; the pain returning in week two of the return
- **Alpha's actual position, stated plainly:** rest has a place, but it is rarely the whole answer. Rest removes the load that hurt you. It doesn't change the thing that made the load hurt.
- Which is why the useful question is not *what hurts* but *why does it hurt when you run and not when you walk*
- Do not disparage whoever gave the advice — attack the advice, not the adviser
- Land the North Star: root of the problem

### 3. What the assessment actually involves
**Component:** `approach-steps` — **the page's core, and the section that sells it**
**Direction:** **Be specific to the point of being almost technical.** Vagueness here is the difference between a $200 booking and a bounce. This reader wants to know exactly what she's paying for. Every item must be verified as something Alpha genuinely does — see Open Questions.
**Key points:**
- **The conversation first.** Training history, current volume, the block you're in, what you're training for, injury history, footwear, surfaces, what you've already tried. Most of what explains a running injury is in the history, not the video.
- **Physical assessment off the treadmill.** Joint range, single-leg strength and control, calf and hamstring capacity, hip and glute strength, ankle stiffness, foot mechanics, load tolerance testing.
- **Video gait analysis on the treadmill.** Multiple angles, slow-motion, at your actual paces — not one arbitrary speed. Name what's being looked at: cadence, foot strike and contact time, pelvic control, knee position, trunk position, arm carriage, asymmetry side to side.
- **Watching how it changes when you're tired**, if relevant. Form at kilometre 2 and form at kilometre 20 are different problems, and this is the detail that signals genuine expertise.
- **Training load review.** Volume, progression rate, intensity distribution, recovery, sleep, what changed in the weeks before the pain started. Frequently the actual answer.
- **Footwear.** Honest, non-commercial — what you're in, whether it suits, and no shoes for sale at the end of it. Worth stating explicitly given who the reader is comparing you against.
- **The debrief.** You see the footage. It gets explained. You leave understanding what's happening — playbook education-first, applied.
- **What you take away:** a written plan — the specific strength work, the load and progression plan, the technique cues if any are warranted, and a review point.

### 4. What it costs and how long it takes
**Component:** dedicated block, plain and unapologetic
**Direction:** Cost transparency is a brief requirement. **Do not hedge, do not say "from", do not send people to a phone call.** The playbook is explicit that Alpha holds its pricing and educates on value rather than discounting — so state the price and justify it in the same breath.
**Key points:**
- Duration (confirm — likely 60–75 minutes)
- Price, stated
- Whether a private health rebate applies and under which item — **material to the decision, and confirm before publishing**
- What's included and whether a follow-up or review is part of it or separate
- What to bring: your usual running shoes (and any others you rotate), running kit, your training data if you have it (Strava, Garmin, a spreadsheet — say so, this reader has it)
- **One honest paragraph on value**, in the playbook's register: this is a longer appointment with more assessment and a written plan, and it's priced accordingly. No apology, no discount, no "investment in yourself" language.

### 5. Not injured? This is still for you
**Component:** dedicated content block with its own H2
**Direction:** **Required by the brief and commercially under-exploited.** Most competitors ignore this reader entirely. Give her a real section, not a sentence.
**Key points:**
- Runners who book without pain: starting a first half or marathon block; returning after a long layoff; stepping up volume significantly; changing surface or shoe type; masters runners wanting to keep going; anyone who has had the same injury twice and doesn't want a third
- What she gets that's different: a baseline, a strength profile, a load progression plan for the block ahead, and the specific weaknesses worth working on before they cost her
- **This is the honest version of injury prevention.** Do not use the word "prevent" — see AHPRA notes. "Understanding where you're likely to run into trouble" is accurate and permissible.
- Postnatal return to running belongs here too → link /conditions/postpartum-pain/

### 6. What we commonly find
**Component:** short structured list
**Direction:** Concrete, credible, and it makes the assessment feel real rather than abstract. Frame as patterns, not diagnoses.
**Key points:**
- Load progression that outran the tissue's capacity — the most common finding by a distance
- Calf and foot capacity that hasn't kept up with the volume
- Single-leg control and pelvic stability under fatigue
- Cadence and overstriding — with the honest caveat that changing cadence is a tool, not a universal fix, and gets used selectively
- Hip and glute strength
- Old injuries that never got rehabbed properly and left an asymmetry
- Sleep, stress and recovery — genuinely part of load, and rarely mentioned by anyone else

## AHPRA Compliance Notes

- **Do not use "prevent", "injury prevention" or "prevents injury"** anywhere on the page, including headings and meta. This is the specific temptation of section 5. Use "understand where you're likely to run into trouble", "reduce the things that make injury more likely", "build a plan for the block ahead".
- No performance claims — no "run faster", "improve your PB", "optimise your running". This is doubly barred: it is an outcome claim *and* it collides with the founder's flagged word.
- No claim that gait retraining fixes or resolves any specific injury.
- **If a price is published it must be accurate and current**, and any rebate statement must be verifiable. Incorrect health-fund claims are a consumer-law problem as well as an AHPRA one.
- Testimonials: experience-of-care only. "I ran a PB after this" is prohibited.
- No before/after video of gait presented as a therapeutic result.
- Any assessment technology named must be described accurately — no implying clinical-grade motion capture if it is a phone on a tripod. (Which is fine, and should be described as what it is.)

## Voice Notes

- **The most confident page in the batch.** This is Alpha's home ground and the tone can carry the clinic's full energy.
- Write like someone who runs. Use the vocabulary correctly — blocks, cadence, easy runs, threshold, taper, DOMS, niggle. Getting this wrong is instantly detectable to this reader; getting it right buys enormous credibility.
- Specific over impressive. "We film from three angles at your easy and threshold paces" beats "comprehensive biomechanical analysis" every time.
- **Never "performance".** Never "optimise". "Getting back to what you love", "getting through the block", "still running at 50".
- Don't disparage shoe shops, YouTube, or the physio who said rest. Be better, visibly, and say nothing about them.
- No hedging on price. Confidence about cost is on-brand and is explicitly the playbook's position.

## Open Questions

1. **Blocking — the actual protocol.** Every item in section 3 must be verified. Specifically: is there a treadmill? How many camera angles? What software? Is the video reviewed live with the patient or after? Is there a written report, and is it given to every patient? **The page's entire value proposition rests on this being accurate, and it cannot be written from assumption.**
2. **Blocking — price and duration.** Both are required on the page and neither is documented.
3. **Blocking — which clinics offer it?** If treadmill and video are only at one site, the booking block must say so.
4. Does a private health rebate apply, and under what item number / practitioner type? Must be verified before publishing any rebate claim.
5. Is a follow-up or review included in the fee or booked separately?
6. Who delivers the assessments, and do they run themselves? Section 9 is much stronger if the answer is yes and it can be named.
7. Does Alpha sell or fit shoes or orthotics? Determines the FAQ answer and the independence positioning.
8. Is there a postnatal return-to-running variant of this assessment? Flagged in the postpartum brief as a commercial opportunity — **needs one decision covering both pages.**
9. Can the clinic supply assessment footage or photography for the hero? Stock imagery would materially weaken this page.
