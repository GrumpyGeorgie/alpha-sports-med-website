## Purpose

Take the highest-volume treatment term on the entire site (14,800/mo, under a 90,500/mo head term), and convert a reader who has had heel pain for months, has bought insoles and a night splint, and has started to believe it's permanent.

## Reader

Overwhelmingly one dominant presentation with a very distinctive story, plus a general foot-pain tail.

**The plantar heel pain reader:** sharp pain under the heel on the first steps out of bed, which eases after a few minutes of walking, then returns after sitting, and is worst at the end of a long day on their feet. Duration is typically months. They have already tried: insoles from the chemist, a rolled frozen water bottle, a golf ball, calf stretches, a night splint, new shoes, and rest. Some of it helped a bit. None of it held.

Emotional state: worn down and increasingly fatalistic. They've read that it can last years, which is the thing they most want contradicted, and which cannot be honestly contradicted outright — only reframed.

Two sub-populations worth writing for: the runner (whose mileage triggered it) and the person who spends all day standing at work (nurse, teacher, hospitality, trades, retail), who cannot modify their load at all and is rarely written for.

**Secondary readers:** general foot pain — arch pain, ball-of-foot pain, Achilles and back-of-heel pain, big toe pain.

Five-second need: *there's a reason none of that worked, and there's more to try.*

### 4. It's not really inflammation (and it's not a heel spur)
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** **The page's intellectual centre**, and it clears up two pieces of misinformation that shape everything this reader has done so far.
**Key points:**
- The "-itis" implies inflammation; persistent plantar heel pain is better understood as a degenerative and load-related change in the tissue. Many clinicians now call it plantar heel pain or plantar fasciopathy for that reason.
- **Why this matters practically:** it explains why anti-inflammatories, ice and rest give temporary relief and don't hold. The reader's own experience becomes the evidence.
- **Heel spurs:** extremely common in people with no pain, frequently absent in people with pain, and generally not the cause. If they've been told a spur is causing it, this is genuinely liberating information — the spur is a bystander.
- What's actually happening: the tissue under the foot is being asked to tolerate more than it currently can. Every day. Because you walk.
- Frame mechanisms as current understanding, cautiously.
- 300–350 words.

### 5. Why the stretching and the insoles didn't fix it
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** **The conversion section.** Address every attempted fix by name. The reader has tried them all and nobody has explained why they fell short.
**Key points:**
- Calf and plantar stretching — helps some symptoms, doesn't build tissue tolerance
- Rolling a ball or a frozen bottle — genuine short-term relief, no lasting change
- Off-the-shelf insoles — can offload and reduce symptoms for some people, and that's a legitimate use. They support the tissue rather than strengthen it, so symptoms often return when they come out.
- Night splints — can help morning symptoms, tolerated by relatively few people
- Rest — the same trap as every other tendon-like tissue: pain drops, capacity drops, resumption reproduces it
- New shoes — sometimes help, and no shoe fixes a capacity problem
- Cortisone — may give short-term relief; there are trade-offs and it's a decision for the treating doctor. Neutral framing, no campaigning.
- **The common thread: almost everything on that list reduces load or reduces symptoms. Almost nothing on it builds the foot's capacity — and that's the gap.**

### 6. What actually changes it
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** The treatment argument. Concrete enough to show competence, general enough not to be a prescription.
**Key points:**
- Work out what changed: a jump in running volume, a new job on your feet, a house renovation, a holiday of walking, weight change, a period in flat unsupportive shoes, a return to training after time off
- Settle symptoms so you can load — hands-on treatment, taping, temporary footwear or insole changes, and honest short-term load reduction
- **Progressive loading of the foot and calf** — the primary driver of change. Heavy, slow, progressed over months. Say directly that this is the piece almost everyone is missing.
- Calf capacity specifically: the calf complex absorbs an enormous amount of the load the heel would otherwise take
- Load management for people who cannot reduce load: shift patterns, footwear at work, standing mats, what to do on days off
- **The timeline, honestly:** plantar heel pain typically responds over months, not weeks. Improvement usually shows as fewer bad mornings before it shows as no bad mornings — give the reader that as the marker of progress, because it's the thing that keeps people going.
- Refer on where warranted: persistent cases, atypical presentations, or where imaging or a podiatry/orthotic opinion is appropriate

### 7. Other foot pain we see
**Component:** `condition-cards`
**Direction:** The secondary reader and the long-tail. Four presentations, 60–100 words each. Keep them short — plantar heel pain is the page's job.
**Key points:**
- **Achilles and back-of-heel pain** — different location, different problem, links to /conditions/sports-injuries/tendonitis-tendinopathy/
- **Ball-of-foot pain** — forefoot load, common in runners and in anyone in narrow or unsupportive shoes; nerve-related causes mentioned briefly

### 8. Shoes and orthotics, honestly
**Component:** standard content

- A shoe that reduces symptoms is doing something useful — that's not cheating
- Custom orthotics can genuinely help some people, particularly alongside a loading program, and they're rarely the whole answer on their own
- Big, sudden changes in footwear — including into minimalist or zero-drop shoes — are a common trigger and worth managing carefully

## Voice Notes

- The reader is worn down and slightly fatalistic. The tone is calm competence plus a genuine reason for optimism — not cheerleading.
