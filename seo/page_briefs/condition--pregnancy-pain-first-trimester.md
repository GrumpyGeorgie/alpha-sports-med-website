## Purpose

Serve the early-pregnancy reader whose real question is not "how do I treat this pain" but **"am I allowed to do anything about this yet, and will it hurt the baby"** — and hold the existing `/pregnancy-pain-first-trimester/` equity while doing it.

> ⚠ **Read the Open Questions before writing.** This is the weakest of the three trimester pages and the one most at risk of being thin content. There is a documented fallback recommendation. Do not paper over it.

## Reader

Weeks 1–13. Very often she has told almost nobody. That single fact shapes the entire page: she is researching alone, cannot ask her workplace for modifications, and is reluctant to book anything that requires explaining why.

She is exhausted in a way she did not anticipate, possibly nauseated for most of the day, and the pain she has is usually **not new** — it's the back or pelvic problem she already had, which has become much harder to manage now that she can't take her usual anti-inflammatories, can't train the way she did, and is running on no energy.

There is also a smaller, distinct group: people with a **history of pregnancy loss**, for whom every twinge is terrifying and every decision is high-stakes.

**First five seconds must deliver:** early pregnancy pain is common, most of it is not a warning sign, here is the short list of things that *are*, and yes you can be treated now — with these specific modifications.

## Search Intent

Predominantly **informational and reassurance-seeking**, not commercial. This is the key difference from the other two trimester pages and it should change how the page is written: the reader is not shopping for a clinic yet. She is checking whether she's allowed to.

**There is no verified head term.** The traffic here is a long tail of anxious phrasings — "back pain 8 weeks pregnant", "is it ok to see a physio in early pregnancy", "pelvic pain early pregnancy not miscarriage". The page wins by being genuinely useful to a worried person, and converts later, in the second trimester, when she comes back.

## What makes this page genuinely different

State this plainly for the writer, because the failure mode is writing the hub again with "first trimester" swapped in. **Nothing below appears on the hub or the other two trimester pages:**

1. **Safety and permission is the whole page.** On the second and third trimester pages, safety is one section. Here it is the spine of the document.
2. **The pain is usually pre-existing, not pregnancy-caused.** The clinical framing is different — this is management of an existing problem under new constraints, not a new presentation.
3. **The constraints are pharmacological and behavioural, not mechanical.** Nothing is physically in the way yet. What has changed is what she can take, how much energy she has, and what she's frightened of.
4. **Nausea and fatigue are the dominant symptoms**, and they drive musculoskeletal problems nobody connects — neck and upper back pain from hours hunched over, jaw and neck tension from retching, rib pain, deconditioning within weeks.
5. **The red flags are different and more urgent.** Ectopic pregnancy and miscarriage sit in this window and nowhere else in the cluster. This changes the escalation block completely.
6. **The exercise conversation is about maintaining, not modifying.** She is being told by the internet to stop everything. She mostly doesn't have to.

### 2. What's actually going on in the first 13 weeks
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** ~120 words. Physiology first, because it is the thing that makes the pain make sense — and it is genuinely stage-specific.
**Key points:**
- Relaxin and progesterone rise from very early — well before anything is visible. Ligament laxity starts in weeks, not months. This surprises almost everyone.
- Blood volume and cardiovascular demand climb early; fatigue is physiological, not weakness
- Nothing is mechanically in the way yet — which is why most first-trimester pain traces back to something that was already there
- Validate the specific isolation of this stage: pain, exhaustion, and often nobody at work knows

### 3. What we see in early pregnancy
**Component:** structured list, 4–5 items, ~50 words each
**Direction:** These must be first-trimester-specific presentations, not the hub's list.
**Key points:**
- **A back or pelvic problem you already had, now harder to manage** — the most common presentation by a distance. No NSAIDs, less sleep, less training, more fatigue.
- **Neck, upper back and jaw pain from nausea** — hours spent hunched, retching, or lying still in one position. Rarely connected by the patient. Worth naming explicitly; it is the page's most useful original insight.
- **Low back and pelvic ache from early ligament laxity** — real, and often dismissed as "too early to be pregnancy"
- **Deconditioning pain** — someone who trained four times a week and has done nothing for six weeks. Say it kindly.
- **Round ligament twinges** — can start late in the first trimester; explain what they feel like and why they are not a warning sign. Link forward to the second trimester page where this is covered properly.

### 4. Can I be treated this early?
**Component:** standard content — **the page's centrepiece, give it the most words**
**Direction:** Answer in the first sentence. Then be specific about what changes. Specificity is the reassurance; generalities read as hedging.
**Key points:**
- Yes — pregnancy is disclosed at booking and at the appointment, and treatment is adapted from the first appointment onward
- What changes even this early: no prone lying once it's uncomfortable, no high-velocity manipulation of the lumbar spine or pelvis, gentler dosage, care with abdominal work, no heat modalities over the abdomen or lower back
- **State Alpha's actual first-trimester policy plainly** — see Open Questions, this is blocking
- Tell us if the pregnancy is high-risk, if you've had a previous loss, if you're under specialist care, or if you've been given any activity restriction
- **The boundary:** we treat musculoskeletal pain. Anything about the pregnancy itself is your GP, midwife or early pregnancy service. Cramping and bleeding is never a physio question.
- Reassure on discretion: you do not have to have told anyone else.

### 5. When to contact a doctor or early pregnancy service — not us
**Component:** callout / alert block, visually distinct
**Direction:** **Mandatory. Different from the hub's list and more urgent.** Clinician sign-off required.
**Key points:**
- Vaginal bleeding or spotting with pain
- One-sided lower abdominal or pelvic pain, especially with shoulder-tip pain, dizziness or faintness — **possible ectopic pregnancy, this is an emergency, do not wait**
- Severe cramping
- Inability to keep fluids down / severe persistent vomiting (hyperemesis) — this is treatable and worth seeing someone about, not something to endure
- Fever, or pain on passing urine
- Close with: ring your GP, your early pregnancy assessment service, or Nurse-on-Call. **Confirm exact Victorian pathway wording with the client.**

### 6. Training, running and the gym in the first trimester
**Component:** short content block
**Direction:** Genuinely useful, genuinely on-brand (the "rest isn't the answer" position), and the most-searched sub-question here. Handle with care.
**Key points:**
- Most people who were already active can keep going in some form, but the parameters are set with your GP or midwife — not with us and not with the internet
- What typically changes early: heat, dehydration, intensity when you're exhausted, and being sensible about anything where a fall is likely
- Fatigue is the real limiter in the first trimester, not load
- Deconditioning happens fast and is harder to claw back later — a reason to keep doing something rather than nothing
- Link to /services/exercise-physiology/, /services/clinical-pilates/
