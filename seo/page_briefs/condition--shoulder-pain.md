## Purpose

Take treatment intent under an 8,100/mo head term and convert the reader who can't sleep on that side, can't reach overhead, and has been told they have a rotator cuff tear on a scan.

## Reader

Two dominant populations.

1. **The 40-to-60 gradual-onset shoulder.** No injury. Started as a niggle reaching behind, now can't sleep on it, can't reach a seatbelt or a top shelf. Often has a scan showing a rotator cuff tear or "impingement" and is frightened by it. Sleep disruption is the thing that finally makes them book — night pain is the most reliable conversion driver on this page.
2. **The training shoulder.** Gym-goer, swimmer, thrower, climber. Pain with pressing or overhead work. Wants to keep training.

Both share a fear of surgery and a suspicion that it's structural and therefore permanent.

Five-second need: *a scan finding isn't a sentence, and this can be treated without surgery in most cases.*

### 3. What we see most

**Key points:**

### 4. About that scan
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** **The page's highest-value section**, and the same argument as the back pain page applied to a different structure. Reader 1 is frightened by a report and it is changing their behaviour.
**Key points:**
- Rotator cuff tears are extremely common in people with no shoulder pain at all, and become more common with age. Many are age-related change rather than injury.
- A tear on a scan does not automatically mean the tear is causing the pain, and it does not automatically mean surgery
- The essential qualifier again: this doesn't mean the pain isn't real
- What tends to matter more clinically than the scan: what you can and can't do, how it responds to loading, and how it changes over time
- When surgery genuinely is part of the conversation — traumatic full-thickness tears in younger people, recurrent instability, and shoulders not responding to a proper course of rehab. Say this. Balance is what makes the reassurance credible.

### 5. Night pain
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** Short, practical, high-value. Almost nobody writes this and it's the symptom that gets people out of bed and onto Google at 2am.
**Key points:**
- Why shoulders hurt more at night — reduced distraction, sustained positions, compression of the sore side
- Practical positioning: which side, pillow under the arm, pillow behind the back if you roll
- The honest note: night pain often improves as the shoulder does, and persistent severe night pain that isn't shifting is worth reviewing
- Frame as management, not a fix

### 6. How we treat shoulder pain
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** The Alpha process. Emphasise progressive loading — shoulders respond to it and most people are given a resistance band and abandoned.
**Key points:**
- Assess the shoulder, the shoulder blade, the neck and the thoracic spine — and ask what you're actually trying to get back to
- Hands-on treatment to reduce pain and restore movement so loading becomes possible
- Progressive strengthening — the primary driver of change for most shoulder pain, and typically the missing piece. Be direct that a band and three exercises is not a program.
- Modify rather than stop training: change range, angle, grip or load rather than abandoning pressing entirely
- Timeframes honestly: rotator cuff-related shoulder pain typically responds over months rather than weeks, and frozen shoulder longer again
- Refer for imaging or a specialist opinion when it's warranted, and say when it would be
