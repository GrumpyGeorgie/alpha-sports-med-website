## Purpose

Own `postpartum back pain` (260/mo, competition 0.01 — one of the softest terms in the entire keyword map) and be the page that takes seriously the pain everybody told her would go away on its own.

## Reader

Anywhere from six weeks to four years after birth. That range is not a mistake — it is the defining feature of this reader. **A large share of the people who land here are two years postpartum and still don't feel right**, and they have stopped calling it a pregnancy problem because it feels too late to.

She presents in one of three ways:

1. **Early (6 weeks – 6 months).** Neck, upper back and wrist pain from feeding and carrying. Pelvic pain that didn't go away with the birth like she was told it would. Exhausted, often not sleeping, and carrying between 4 and 9 kilos for large parts of the day.
2. **Returning to exercise (3–12 months).** Wants to run again, or lift again, and either something hurts or she's been told vaguely to "be careful". The most commercially valuable segment and the one where Alpha's whole model actually fits.
3. **Long-tail (1–4+ years).** Persistent back or pelvic pain, or leaking, that has quietly become normal. She has usually been told at her six-week check that everything was fine, and she has believed for years that the window closed.

Across all three: **she is not the priority in her own life right now.** Booking anything for herself takes effort, guilt and childcare. The page must make that easy and must never imply she should have come sooner.

**First five seconds must deliver:** this is treatable, it is not too late, and it does not matter how long it's been.

## Search Intent

Commercial, with the lowest competition-to-value ratio in the batch. The SERP for `postpartum back pain` is national informational content — a local clinic page with real clinical depth and named practitioners can win it.

The higher-value long-tail sits around **return to running and return to lifting after birth**, which is where Alpha's exercise-integrated model is genuinely differentiated and where the running cluster cross-links pay off.

### 2. "It's just what happens after a baby"
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** ~160 words. The strongest validation section on the site alongside Invisible Illnesses. Recognition, not sympathy.
**Key points:**
- Name the daily reality: the neck pain from feeding, the wrist pain from lifting a car capsule, the back that goes at bath time, the pelvis that still isn't right, the leak when you sneeze that you've stopped mentioning
- Name the message she's absorbed — that this is the cost of having a baby and there's nothing to be done. Attack the *idea*, never the person who said it.
- **"It's been two years, is it too late?" — answer it here, explicitly, in the second paragraph. No.** Persistent postnatal symptoms respond to assessment and loading work regardless of how long they have been there.
- Acknowledge the practical barrier honestly: booking something for yourself with a small child is genuinely hard. Then remove it — pram-friendly rooms, babies welcome in the appointment, appointment times, whatever is actually true.
- Land the North Star as *getting back to what you love* — running, lifting, picking your kid up without bracing.

### 3. What we see most often
**Component:** `condition-cards` / structured list, ~70 words each
**Direction:** Problem-led, in the order she'd recognise them.
**Key points:**
- **Neck, upper back and shoulder pain** — feeding positions, hours of static holding, sleeping in weird positions. The most common presentation and the one least often connected to the baby.
- **Wrist and thumb pain** — De Quervain's / "mother's thumb" from the scooping-a-baby grip. Extremely common, extremely under-treated, and she will recognise it instantly. Link /conditions/hand-wrist-pain/
- **Low back pain** — new loading patterns, prolonged carrying, floor time, an abdominal wall that isn't back to doing its job
- **Pelvic and pubic symphysis pain that didn't resolve** — the group that was told it would go away at birth and it didn't. Link up to /conditions/pregnancy-pain/#pubic-symphysis-pain
- **Abdominal separation (DRAM)** — what it is, what actually matters (pressure management and function, not the size of the gap), and what can be done. **No claims about closure or "healing the gap".**
- **Pelvic floor symptoms** — leaking, heaviness, pain. **Scope carefully — see Open Questions.** Whatever Alpha's scope is, name it honestly and provide a referral pathway to a pelvic health physiotherapist where it sits outside.
- **Scar-related discomfort** — caesarean and perineal. Only include if within confirmed scope; otherwise refer.

### 4. It doesn't matter how long it's been
**Component:** dedicated content block with its own H2
**Direction:** **The page's conversion section.** Deserves its own heading rather than being folded into section 2, because it is also the search-visible answer to "is it too late" queries.
**Key points:**
- The six-week check is a screen, not a discharge. "No concerns" at six weeks does not mean nothing can be improved at two years.
- Tissue healing timelines and symptom timelines are different things. Persistent pain is usually about load, strength and control — all of which are trainable at any point.
- Second and third babies change the picture again
- Direct: if you've been carrying this for years, that is the most common story we hear, not an unusual one.

### 5. Getting back to running, lifting and training
**Component:** standard content — **give this real weight**
**Direction:** Alpha's clearest differentiator on this page and the highest-value segment. This is the exercise-integrated model in its natural habitat. Concrete over reassuring.
**Key points:**
- The generic advice she's had ("wait twelve weeks", "just be careful") is not a plan. Say what a plan looks like instead.
- What gets assessed before returning to running: load tolerance, single-leg strength and control, pelvic floor symptoms under load, how the abdominal wall manages pressure, sleep and overall load
- Progressive return: walking → load tolerance → impact, built around a body that is sleep-deprived and carrying a baby all day
- Lifting and group fitness: what to change and for how long
- **Timelines are individual.** Do not publish a week-by-week protocol as though it applies to everyone.
- Nothing here is contingent on how the birth went — caesarean and vaginal birth both get assessed, differently
- Links: /conditions/running-injuries/, /running-assessment/, /services/exercise-physiology/, /services/strength-conditioning/, /services/clinical-pilates/, /runwest-run-club/

### 6. How we work with you
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Key points:**
- A proper first appointment with time to explain the whole picture — birth, feeding, sleep, what you're lifting, what you want back
- Assessment of how you're actually loading day to day, not just where it hurts
- Hands-on treatment where it helps
- Strength and control work built around a real day with a baby in it — short, realistic, doable one-handed
- Practical ergonomics: feeding set-up, carrying, prams, capsules, floor transfers
- Referral on where something sits outside scope — pelvic health physiotherapy, GP, women's health specialist. **Name this as a strength, not a caveat.**
- Babies welcome. Say it plainly.

### 7. When to see your GP or a doctor
**Component:** callout block
**Direction:** Shorter than the pregnancy blocks but still mandatory and still clinician-signed-off.
**Key points:**
- Heavy or increasing bleeding, foul-smelling discharge, fever — urgent
- Severe headache, visual disturbance or swelling in the weeks after birth (postnatal pre-eclampsia is real and commonly missed)
- Calf pain or swelling, chest pain, breathlessness — emergency
- New numbness or weakness in the legs, saddle numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control — emergency
- Wound problems, or pain that is severe and escalating
- **Mental health, stated warmly and without hedging:** if you're not okay, that matters as much as the pain. Talk to your GP or maternal and child health nurse. Include PANDA (1300 726 306) and Lifeline (13 11 14). **This is not optional content on a postpartum page.**

## AHPRA Compliance Notes

- Inherits the pregnancy hub guardrails.
- **Prohibited:** any claim that treatment closes, repairs or heals abdominal separation; any claim about "restoring" the body; any before/after imagery of an abdomen — this is the highest-risk image category on the site and must be excluded from the design system for this page.
- No "bounce back", "get your body back", "pre-baby body". These are brand-damaging as well as claims-adjacent.
- No claims about resolving incontinence. "Pelvic floor symptoms are common and often respond to assessment and targeted work" is the ceiling, and only if it is within confirmed scope.
- **Scope of practice is the live risk on this page.** If Alpha does not perform internal pelvic floor assessment, the page must not imply pelvic floor expertise it doesn't have. An honest referral pathway is stronger positioning than an overclaim and much safer.
- Testimonials: experience-of-care only. "I can run again" is an outcome claim.
- Mental health content must signpost only — no assessment or advice, clear referral to GP/PANDA/Lifeline.
- Named clinician sign-off on section 7 and on the return-to-running content.
