## Purpose

Convert the patient who can't get to a clinic — by distance, schedule, illness or travel — and answer the honest question of whether an appointment without hands-on treatment is worth having.

## Reader

Four situations, all defined by access rather than preference.

(1) The **existing patient** who has moved, is travelling, is sick, or can't make the drive this week and doesn't want to lose momentum in a programme.
(2) The **regional or interstate** patient — including outer-west and beyond-Bacchus-Marsh residents for whom the drive is the barrier.
(3) The **time-poor** professional or parent for whom a lunchtime video call is the only realistic option.
(4) The **rehab-phase** patient whose treatment is now mostly exercise progression and coaching, where hands-on adds little.

All of them are sceptical about the same thing: *what's the point without hands-on treatment?* The page lives or dies on how well it answers that.

## Search Intent

Commercial, volume estimated. Post-pandemic, telehealth search demand normalised rather than disappeared, and the searcher is usually specific about discipline ("online physio", "telehealth exercise physiology"). Verify volume and consider whether the primary should be `online physio consultation` or a broader telehealth term.

Note the searcher is often solving a constraint rather than shopping — which makes them high-intent and low-comparison.

### 2. "What's the point without hands-on treatment?"
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** **The page's central question and its best section.** Answer it with unusual honesty — including what telehealth genuinely can't do. Salvage the argument from the retiring telehealth article, but reframe it: the original was written to justify telehealth during a lockdown, and it needs to be rewritten as a settled, practical service.
**Key points:**
- The uncomfortable truth that makes the argument work: most of the value in an appointment isn't the hands-on part. It's the history, the assessment, the explanation and the plan. Hands-on treatment helps symptoms; the plan is what changes the outcome.
- What works well online: assessment through guided movement, diagnosis for most musculoskeletal presentations, exercise prescription and technique coaching, load and training-plan management, progress reviews, return-to-sport decisions, education
- What doesn't: hands-on treatment, obviously — plus some assessments that need physical testing, and anything where you'd need hands to be confident
- Say plainly: if we think you need to be seen in person, we'll tell you in the appointment and help you arrange it
- Note that some patients do a hybrid — in-clinic for treatment, online for the coaching and progression sessions in between

## AHPRA Compliance Notes

- Do not claim telehealth is equivalent to in-person care. Describe what it does well and what it doesn't — the honest framing in section 2 is also the compliant one.
- Rebate claims must be conditional. Telehealth extras cover varies by fund and by discipline; a flat "claimable on private health" statement would be misleading.
- Medicare telehealth allied health rules are specific and change — either state them precisely and date-check them, or say "ask us" and leave it.
- Practitioners must be practising within their registration and within the jurisdiction rules for interstate patients. Do not advertise a service the practitioners aren't positioned to deliver.
- Privacy: video consultations involve health information: reference /privacy-policy/ and confirm the platform is compliant.
