## Purpose

Own the weeks 14–27 window, where most pregnancy pain actually begins and where the reader is most likely to book — and anchor the page on **round ligament pain**, the one genuinely searchable, genuinely stage-specific condition in the trimester set.

## Reader

Weeks 14–27. The nausea has usually lifted, she has told people, she has energy back, and she has made plans — and then something started hurting. That sequence matters: **this reader was feeling good and has just been interrupted.** Her frustration is different from the first-trimester reader's fear.

She is also the reader most likely to have had a sharp, frightening pain low down in her abdomen when she stood up quickly or rolled over, gone straight to her phone, and read something alarming. Round ligament pain is the single most Googled scare of this trimester.

By late second trimester a second theme arrives: she can no longer sleep, sit, drive or train the way she has been, and the workarounds she's been using have stopped working.

**First five seconds must deliver:** this is when this stuff usually starts, the sharp pulling pain is almost certainly not what you're afraid of, and there is a lot that can be done about the rest of it.

## Search Intent

Informational leading into commercial. **This is the strongest of the three trimester pages** — it is the only one with a plausible standalone head term (`round ligament pain`, an established, high-search-volume symptom term with a distinct name), and it is the trimester in which people actually start booking.

Structure it so that the round ligament section could stand alone as a snippet-winning answer block, with the rest of the page catching the broader "second trimester back pain" and "pelvic girdle pain" traffic that the hub deep-links down to.

## What makes this page genuinely different

**Nothing below belongs on the hub, the first trimester page or the third:**

1. **Round ligament pain is a second-trimester condition.** It has a name, a mechanism, a distinctive presentation (sharp, brief, one-sided, movement-triggered), and a very specific reassurance job. This alone justifies the page.
2. **This is when pelvic girdle pain typically starts.** The mechanism is now genuinely different from the first trimester — real load, real centre-of-mass change, real ligament laxity, not just pre-existing pain under new constraints.
3. **The mechanical constraints begin.** Prone lying stops. Late in this trimester, supine positioning starts to become uncomfortable and is generally modified from around 16–20 weeks onward. Treatment positioning changes here, and that is a concrete, page-specific thing to explain.
4. **Rib and upper back pain from ribcage flare** starts in this window as the thorax accommodates.
5. **DRAM / abdominal separation becomes a live topic** — usually the first time she hears the term, usually from a group chat, usually with bad information attached.
6. **The exercise conversation shifts from "can I" to "how do I change it"** — she is still training, and the modification conversation is concrete and practical rather than permission-seeking.
7. **Fetal movement red flags become relevant** from around 24 weeks. They do not exist on the first-trimester page.

### 2. Why things start hurting around now
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** ~130 words. The mechanical story, told plainly. This is the "aha" that most competitor content skips.
**Key points:**
- The bump becomes load-bearing in this window: centre of mass moves forward and up, the lumbar curve increases, the pelvis takes a different line of force
- Ligament laxity that began in the first trimester now has something to act on
- The abdominal wall is lengthening and doing less of its usual stabilising job — which is why the back and pelvis take more
- Validate the frustration specifically: you finally felt normal again and now this. That's the usual order, not bad luck.

### 3. Round ligament pain
**Component:** dedicated content block with its own H2 and anchor (`#round-ligament-pain`)
**Direction:** **The page's flagship section. Write it as a standalone, snippet-ready answer.** Lead with a clean definition paragraph (40–55 words) that could be lifted directly into a featured snippet or an AI answer, then explain.
**Key points:**
- What it is: the round ligaments run from the uterus to the groin and stretch as the uterus grows; sudden movement can pull them sharply
- **What it feels like** — sharp, stabbing or pulling; low down, on one or both sides; typically brief (seconds); triggered by standing up quickly, rolling over, coughing, sneezing, laughing
- **Why it is frightening and why it usually isn't dangerous** — say this directly and early
- **What distinguishes it from something that needs urgent review:** round ligament pain is brief and movement-linked. Pain that is constant, escalating, rhythmic, one-sided and persistent, or accompanied by bleeding, fever or fluid loss is a different thing and needs your maternity team **now**. Do not soften this contrast.
- What helps: pacing the movements that trigger it, changing how you get out of bed and out of the car, support, hands-on work on the surrounding soft tissue, gentle movement
- Honest framing: it typically settles as the pregnancy progresses. We can make the surrounding load easier to manage; we don't stop the ligament stretching.

### 4. Pelvic girdle pain and pubic symphysis pain
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** Second trimester is usually the onset window. **Do not restate the hub** — cover onset, early recognition, and what to change now to avoid the third trimester being worse. Deep-link to the hub's `#pubic-symphysis-pain` anchor for the full explanation.
**Key points:**
- What early PGP feels like and the single-leg tasks that provoke it (stairs, dressing standing up, getting out of the car, rolling in bed)
- The practical asymmetry rule people find genuinely useful: keep the legs together where you can — swing both legs out of the car, sit down to put pants on, take stairs one at a time
- Why catching it now matters — the load only increases from here
- Belts and taping: sometimes helpful, individual, worth trying properly rather than guessing
- Link: hub anchor, /conditions/hip-pain/, /conditions/back-pain/

### 5. Rib pain, upper back pain and breathlessness
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** Genuinely under-served and specific to this window onward. Short and useful.
**Key points:**
- The ribcage widens and the diaphragm's position changes; the band of pain under the bra line is extremely common
- Bra fit and posture matter more than people expect — practical, non-clinical, and appreciated
- Mild breathlessness on exertion is common in pregnancy; **sudden breathlessness, chest pain or calf pain and swelling is not — that is an emergency.** State it plainly.
- Hands-on work through the thoracic spine and ribs is well tolerated in side-lying

### 6. Abdominal separation (DRAM) — what it actually is
**Component:** short content block
**Direction:** Correct the misinformation without being smug about it. This is the moment she first hears the term.
**Key points:**
- Some separation of the abdominal wall happens in every pregnancy. It is a normal adaptation, not an injury or a failure.
- It is not caused by doing the wrong exercise, and it is not prevented by avoiding all abdominal work
- What matters more than the gap is how the abdominal wall manages pressure
- What to do now: keep moving, work on breathing and load management, and stop doom-scrolling before-and-after photos
- Full assessment and management belongs after birth — link to /conditions/postpartum-pain/
- **Do not promise closure, prevention or repair.**

### 7. Training and exercise from 14 to 27 weeks
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** The concrete modification conversation. This is the section that separates Alpha from generic pregnancy content and it should read like a practitioner talking, not a pamphlet.
**Key points:**
- Positions: prone work stops; from around 16–20 weeks flat-on-back work is generally modified — incline, side-lying or standing variations instead
- Load and impact: many runners keep running into this trimester and beyond; some find pelvic symptoms make it not worth it. Neither outcome is a failure.
- Intra-abdominal pressure: what coning or doming looks like and what to change when you see it
- Heat, hydration, and knowing when fatigue means stop
- Balance and centre-of-mass changes — a practical, unpatronising note
- **Parameters are set with your maternity team.** We adapt the plan; they set the medical limits.
- Link: /services/exercise-physiology/, /services/clinical-pilates/, /services/strength-conditioning/

### 8. When to contact your maternity team instead
**Component:** callout / alert block, visually distinct
**Direction:** Mandatory. Note the additions specific to this trimester.
**Key points:**
- Bleeding, or any fluid loss
- Regular tightenings or contractions before 37 weeks
- **Reduced or changed fetal movements from around 24 weeks — do not wait, do not book us first**
- Severe headache, visual disturbance, sudden swelling of face or hands, pain under the right ribs
- Fever, or pain on passing urine
- Abdominal pain that is constant, severe or escalating rather than brief and movement-linked
- Calf pain or swelling, chest pain, sudden breathlessness — emergency
- Clinician sign-off required.

### 9. What to expect in an appointment now
**Component:** `approach-steps` (abbreviated — 3–4 steps)

**Key points:** Side-lying with pregnancy cushions; no prone; no high-velocity work on the pelvis or lumbar spine; what you'll be given to do at home; how many weeks you are is asked every visit.
