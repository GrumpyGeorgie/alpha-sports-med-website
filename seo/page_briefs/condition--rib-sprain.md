## Purpose

Capture a small but genuinely underserved term, reassure a frightened reader safely, and convert on the specific promise that rib pain is treatable and doesn't have to be waited out.

## Reader

Frightened out of proportion to the injury, and reasonably so — chest pain and breathing pain are alarming, and this reader has almost certainly Googled heart attack and collapsed lung before arriving here. That is the emotional starting point and the page must handle it in the first screen.

Typical origins: a twist in the gym or in a game, a heavy cough or a bout of illness, a fall, sleeping awkwardly, or a rowing/swimming/golf load. Sometimes there's no incident at all, which is more frightening again.

The defining symptom report: sharp, catching, one-sided, worse with a deep breath, sneezing, coughing, laughing, rolling over in bed or reaching overhead.

Five-second need: *is this dangerous.* Answer it before anything else.

## Search Intent

Commercial-leaning with a heavy reassurance overlay. `rib sprain` (320) with essentially zero competition (0.00) — nobody has written this page well, which makes a modest term genuinely winnable. `sharp pain in ribs` type queries sit alongside it and are pure anxiety searches.

Realistic expectation: low volume, high conversion rate. Someone with rib pain who finds a clinic that says "we treat this" books at a much higher rate than average, because most clinics never mention it.

**Component:** alert / callout block
**Direction:** **Above the main hero content.** Chest pain has serious differentials and this page must not be the reason someone doesn't call an ambulance.
**Key points:**

### 4. What a rib sprain actually is
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** **Salvage `/education_hub/sharp-pain-rib-sprain/` here** — it 301s into this page and its explanatory copy is the obvious backbone for this section.
**Key points:**
- Each rib joins the spine at two small joints at the back and connects to the breastbone by cartilage at the front — the ribs move with every breath, which is why nothing about this settles quietly
- A "rib sprain" usually means irritation of one of those joints, or the muscles and soft tissue between the ribs
- Why it's so sharp and so specific: these joints are small, richly innervated, and loaded thousands of times a day
- Why it's worse at night and in bed — rolling loads the joint through range with no muscular protection
- Costochondritis named and briefly explained as the front-of-chest variant
- Note that ribs can also refer pain around the side and into the chest or upper abdomen, which is a large part of why it feels so ominous

### 5. Why it happened
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** Short. Cover the incident and no-incident cases with equal weight, because the no-incident case is the more anxious reader.
**Key points:**
- A twist under load, a rotation in sport, a fall or a knock
- A heavy coughing bout or a long illness — a genuinely common and rarely explained cause
- A stiff mid-back that leaves the ribs doing more movement than they should
- Posture and sustained positions, sleep position
- Sometimes there's no cause to find, and that's not a sign of something sinister

### 6. How we treat it
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** Practical and short. The message is that this responds well and doesn't need to be endured.
**Key points:**
- Assess the rib joints, the mid-back, and the breathing pattern — the second is almost always involved and almost never assessed
- Rule out the things that aren't a rib sprain, and refer on if the picture doesn't fit
- Hands-on treatment to the rib joints and surrounding soft tissue
- Restore mid-back movement so the ribs stop doing the work
- Breathing work — people splint and shallow-breathe to avoid the pain, which perpetuates it
- Gradual return to loading and rotation
- Realistic timeframe: typically settles over a few weeks, and treatment usually aims to make that period more tolerable and get movement back sooner. No promises.
