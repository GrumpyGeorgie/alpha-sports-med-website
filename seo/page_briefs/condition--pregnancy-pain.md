## Purpose

Own the pregnancy treatment cluster (`pregnancy physio` 1,600 + `pregnancy back pain` 1,900) with a hub that answers the two questions every pregnant person actually has — *is this normal, and is it safe to get treated* — and routes them to the right trimester page or straight to a booking.

## Reader

Someone between about 8 and 38 weeks pregnant, most often first pregnancy, searching on a phone, usually at night, usually after a bad day. She has back pain, pelvic pain, or a sharp catch in the front of her pelvis when she rolls over in bed, and she has been told some version of *"that's just pregnancy"* by someone she expected more from.

Three things are true of her at once:

1. **She's in real pain** and has been quietly downplaying it.
2. **She's frightened of making something worse** — not for herself, for the baby. Every decision she makes right now is filtered through that.
3. **She doesn't know this is treatable.** Most pregnancy pain content online is a symptom list and a pillow recommendation. She has low expectations of what any clinic can do.

She is not the playbook's classic 25–50 active patient, but she overlaps heavily with it — a large share of this reader was running or in the gym before pregnancy and has quietly stopped, and getting that back matters to her more than she admits to most people.

**First five seconds must deliver:** yes this is common, no you don't have to put up with it, yes we treat pregnant patients routinely and safely, and here's how to book.

## Search Intent

Commercial with a heavy informational overlay. `pregnancy physio` (1,600, comp 0.20) is a service-seeking search. `pregnancy back pain` (1,900, comp 0.03) is closer to research — but it is unusually low competition for its volume and the SERP is dominated by generic national health content, not clinics. **That is the opening.** A page with genuine clinical detail, named practitioners and a local booking path can out-satisfy a Healthline-style listicle for a Melbourne searcher.

`pubic symphysis pain pregnancy` (260) is the highest-intent term in the set and the most under-served — the person searching it is in significant pain and usually doesn't know the condition has a name. Give it its own named, anchored section on this page.

### 2. "That's just pregnancy" — the validation open
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** **This is the section that converts.** The playbook's "not being listened to" pain point is the single strongest emotional lever on this page. Write it as recognition, not sympathy. ~150 words. Do not trash-talk whoever dismissed her — name the *experience*, not the practitioner.
**Key points:**
- Describe the actual experience in her language: the roll-over pain in bed, the stairs, the catch when getting out of the car, the point in the afternoon where standing stops working
- Pain in pregnancy is extremely common — roughly half of pregnant people report significant back or pelvic pain — and being common is not the same as being untreatable
- Name the trap plainly: because it resolves after birth, it often gets treated as something to endure. Enduring it for six months is not a small thing.
- Land the North Star here: *we get to the root of the problem to unlock your potential* — reframed for this reader as getting through the rest of the pregnancy more comfortably, not "bouncing back"

### 3. What we see most often
**Component:** `condition-cards` or a structured list with anchor IDs
**Direction:** Six named presentations, each 60–90 words, each with a plain-English "what it feels like" line first and the clinical name second. **Order by how often it drives a search, not by clinical hierarchy.** Every one of these needs an anchor ID so the trimester pages and blog posts can deep-link.
**Key points:**
- **Low back pain** — the highest-volume search term in the cluster. Load changes, postural adaptation, and why it often starts earlier than people expect.
- **Pelvic girdle pain (PGP)** — deep ache in the back of the pelvis, one or both sides, worse on single-leg tasks (stairs, dressing, getting out of the car)
- **Pubic symphysis pain / SPD** — sharp pain at the front of the pelvis, grinding or clicking, pain rolling over in bed. **Give this its own heading and anchor (`#pubic-symphysis-pain`).** 260/mo, near-zero competition, and it is the presentation people most often think is abnormal. - **Rib and upper back pain** — ribcage flare, the band of pain under the bra line, common from the second trimester on

- **Wrist and hand pain / carpal tunnel** — fluid-related, typically third trimester, very commonly missed

### 5. Is it safe to be treated while pregnant?
**Component:** standard content block
**Direction:** **The single most-asked question and the biggest conversion blocker on the page.** Answer it directly and specifically, in the first sentence of the section. Vagueness here reads as evasion.
**Key points:**
- Yes, and here is exactly what changes: positioning (side-lying and supported positions, pregnancy cushions, no prone lying past early pregnancy, no flat-on-back work in later pregnancy), technique selection (no high-velocity manipulation of the pelvis or lumbar spine in pregnancy — say this plainly), and pacing
- Practitioners take a full pregnancy history at every appointment: how many weeks, how the pregnancy is going, who is providing your antenatal care, any complications
- **The boundary statement, stated prominently and without hedging:** treatment here is for musculoskeletal pain. It does not replace, duplicate or interfere with the care of your GP, midwife or obstetrician. If anything is going on with the pregnancy itself, that is their call, not ours — and we'll say so.
- If you have a high-risk pregnancy, placenta praevia, are on modified activity, or have any restriction from your maternity team, tell us at booking. We will happily talk to your treating team.
- Do not use the word "safe" as a standalone claim. Use "adapted", "modified", "within these limits".

### 6. When to call your maternity team instead of us
**Component:** callout / alert block — **visually distinct, not a paragraph in the flow**
**Direction:** **Non-negotiable and must appear above the fold on mobile scroll depth, not buried near the FAQ.** This is both a clinical-safety requirement and, as with the paediatric page, the strongest trust signal available. Frame it as care, not as a disclaimer.
**Key points:** Contact your GP, midwife, obstetrician or maternity assessment unit — not a physio, osteo or chiro — if you have:
- Vaginal bleeding, or fluid loss / waters breaking
- Regular tightenings or contractions before 37 weeks
- Reduced or changed fetal movements (from around 24 weeks) — **stated explicitly as "do not wait, do not book us first"**
- Severe or persistent headache, visual disturbance, sudden swelling of face or hands, or pain under the ribs on the right — possible pre-eclampsia
- Fever, or pain on passing urine
- Sudden severe abdominal pain
- New numbness or weakness in the legs, saddle numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control — urgent, any time
- Close with: "If you're not sure, ring your maternity team. That's what they're there for."
- Include the relevant Victorian pathway (maternity assessment unit / PANDA / Nurse-on-Call 1300 60 60 24) — **confirm exact wording with the client**

## AHPRA Compliance Notes

**Second-highest compliance risk on the site after paediatric osteopathy. Do not publish without client sign-off from a named clinician.**

- **Prohibited outright:** any claim or implication that treatment improves birth outcomes, shortens labour, "optimises" fetal position, turns a breech baby, prevents caesarean, or affects the pregnancy itself. This is the specific claim category that attracts regulatory attention in this space. It must not appear in copy, headings, image captions, alt text or testimonials.
- No claim that treatment prevents pregnancy complications or postpartum problems.
- **Testimonials are high-risk here.** A patient saying "I couldn't walk and now I'm fine" is a prohibited outcome claim. If testimonials are used at all, they must be experience-of-care only ("I finally felt listened to", "someone explained what was happening") — and reviewed individually.
- No before/after imagery. No imagery implying a therapeutic result.
- No "specialist" — use "practitioners with additional training in pregnancy-related care".
- Language discipline: "may help", "many of the people we see tell us", "designed to", "we assess". Never "treats", "corrects", "realigns", "resolves".
- Avoid "realign the pelvis" and any structural-correction framing entirely — it is both clinically unsupportable and a claims risk.
- The red-flag block and the "does not replace your maternity care" statement are **mandatory content**, not optional.
- Have a named clinician sign off the clinical content and the red-flag list against current Victorian maternity guidance.
