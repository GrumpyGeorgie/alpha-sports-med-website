## Reader

Rarely a tennis player. Mostly: tradies, desk workers with a mouse-heavy job, new parents lifting a growing baby, gym-goers with grip-heavy training, people who did a weekend of painting or gardening.

Almost never acute. The defining characteristic is duration — this has usually been going for two to six months. They've bought a strap off the internet, tried resting it, tried stretching it, maybe had some massage. It improves and then comes straight back the moment they use the arm. Frustration is the dominant emotion; the fear is that it's now permanent.

Five-second need: *there's an actual reason this isn't getting better, and someone here knows what it is.*

### 3. You probably don't play tennis
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** Open with recognition. The name is misleading and the reader half-suspects they've self-diagnosed wrong. Naming their actual job or activity is the fastest possible trust win.
**Key points:**

- What it feels like: pain on the outside of the elbow, worst when gripping, lifting with the palm down, shaking hands, turning a doorknob, holding a coffee cup or a kettle

### 4. It's not inflammation
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** **The page's intellectual centre and its strongest differentiator.** Almost every competing page still calls this tendinitis and recommends rest and anti-inflammatories. Getting this right is what makes the page credible and explains every treatment decision that follows. **Salvage `/education_hub/what-is-tennis-elbow/` here.**
**Key points:**
- The name says "-itis" but persistent cases are a tendinopathy — a change in the tendon's structure and load tolerance, not an inflammatory process
- Why that matters practically: if it were inflammation, rest and anti-inflammatories would fix it. They don't, which is the reader's lived experience and it should be named as evidence, not contradiction.
- What actually happened: the tendon's capacity to tolerate load dropped below what you're asking of it. Usually after a change — new job, new tool, new baby, new training block, a weekend of DIY.
- Why rest makes it feel better and then worse: the tendon detrains further, so the same task now exceeds an even lower capacity
- Frame mechanisms cautiously — "current understanding is", not "we now know". The tendinopathy model is well supported but the language should stay measured.
- 250–350 words. This is the section that earns the ranking.

### 5. Why the things you've tried didn't work
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** Address each attempted fix by name, without disparaging anyone who suggested it. This is the highest-converting block on the page because it explains the reader's own history back to them.
**Key points:**
- Rest — helps while resting, doesn't build capacity, returns on resumption
- Anti-inflammatories and cortisone — may reduce pain in the short term; the honest framing is that short-term relief and longer-term outcomes are not the same thing, and injection decisions belong with the treating doctor. Do not campaign against cortisone; describe the trade-off neutrally.
- Straps and braces — can offload during specific tasks, genuinely useful as a temporary aid, not a treatment
- Stretching — feels good, doesn't load the tendon
- Massage and dry needling — can help symptoms, insufficient on their own
- The common thread: none of them build load tolerance, and load tolerance is the actual problem

### 6. What actually helps
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** The treatment argument, staged. Be concrete without publishing a program — the aim is to demonstrate competence and make the plan feel specific to them, not to substitute for the appointment.
**Key points:**
- Work out what changed — the load history conversation is the assessment, and it's the bit most practitioners skip
- Modify the aggravating load rather than stopping everything: tool changes, grip changes, mouse and desk setup, technique, how you lift the baby
- Progressive loading of the tendon — isometrics for pain in the early phase, then progressive strengthening, then heavier and faster loading. Describe the principle, not a prescription.
- Look up the chain: the shoulder, scapula, and neck contribute to elbow load more often than people expect, and the neck can refer pain into this area
- Hands-on treatment to make loading tolerable, positioned as an adjunct, not the treatment
- **Timeframes, honestly:** tendons respond slowly. Meaningful change is typically measured in months, not weeks. Say so. This is the single most common reason people abandon rehab that would have worked, and setting the expectation is a clinical intervention in itself.
