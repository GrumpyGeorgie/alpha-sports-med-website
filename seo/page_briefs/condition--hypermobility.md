## Purpose

Make this the best hypermobility page any clinic in Australia has published — because Alpha is one of the very few that can honestly write it. A director lives with the condition, and that lived experience is a genuine, defensible E-E-A-T asset that no competitor can copy.

## Reader

The most under-served reader on the entire site, and the one most likely to become a long-term, high-value patient.

She is typically 20–40, female (though not exclusively — say so), and has spent years being told some version of *"you're just flexible"*, *"you're very bendy!"*, or *"the scans are clear, so there's nothing wrong"*. She has usually seen four or more practitioners. She has often been stretched by someone who should have known better, and felt worse afterwards.

She arrives in one of three states:

1. **Undiagnosed and pattern-matching.** She has just found the word "hypermobility" — often via TikTok, Reddit or a friend — and a dozen separate problems have suddenly connected: the joints that pop out, the fatigue nobody could explain, the dizziness on standing, the gut issues, the anxiety, the bruising. She is exhilarated and terrified in equal measure. **This is the largest group and the reason the 33,100/mo EDS informational cluster matters.**
2. **Diagnosed and stranded.** She has an HSD or hEDS diagnosis and no plan. Diagnosis in Australia is frequently the end of the pathway rather than the start of one. She wants someone who will not be starting from zero.
3. **The athlete who doesn't know yet.** Recurrent shoulder or ankle instability, injuries that keep recurring, "poor proprioception", never quite robust. Nobody has joined the dots. This one overlaps with the running cluster and with Alpha's core sports audience.

**Across all three, the emotional need is identical and it precedes every clinical question: she needs to know she will be believed.**

**First five seconds must deliver:** we know what this is, we take it seriously, and the person who leads this work here has it too.

### 3. What hypermobility actually is
**Component:** standard content with a definition block
**Direction:** Open with a clean 40–55 word definition paragraph suitable for a featured snippet or AI citation. Then go deeper than any competitor. **Assume high health literacy** — this reader has read the diagnostic criteria and will not tolerate being talked down to.
**Key points:**
- Definition block: joints that move beyond typical range; can be localised or generalised
- Connective tissue is not just joints — it is in blood vessels, gut, skin, organs. This single fact is what makes the rest of her experience make sense.
- **Being hypermobile is not, by itself, a problem or a diagnosis.** Plenty of hypermobile people never have symptoms. Say this plainly; it is honest and it builds credibility for everything after it.
- The problem starts when hypermobility comes with pain, recurrent injury, instability, fatigue or dysfunction elsewhere
- Why it is so often missed: the imaging is normal, the presentation is multi-system, and it doesn't fit the restriction-equals-pain model most clinicians are trained in

### 4. Hypermobility vs HSD vs hEDS
**Component:** comparison block / table + explanatory copy
**Direction:** **The page's highest-value informational section.** Nobody local explains this well and the reader is desperate for it. Salvage and expand the version in `seo/2026-03/article-rewrite-hypermobility.md` — it is already accurate and well-written.
**Key points:**
- **Joint hypermobility** — joints move more than expected. No symptoms, no diagnosis needed.
- **Hypermobility Spectrum Disorder (HSD)** — hypermobility plus musculoskeletal symptoms: chronic joint pain, recurrent subluxations, soft tissue injuries, impaired function. Note that HSD replaced "benign joint hypermobility syndrome" — **and say why: there is nothing benign about living in pain.** That line does more trust-building than a paragraph of empathy.
- **Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome (hEDS)** — stricter criteria, systemic connective tissue features, exclusion of other conditions. 13 EDS subtypes; hEDS is the most common and the only one without a confirmed genetic marker.
- **The practically important point, stated clearly:** from a management perspective the HSD/hEDS distinction matters less than people expect. Both can be significantly painful and disabling. Both respond to similar approaches. The distinction is largely for research classification and access to services.
- **Scope boundary, stated explicitly:** Alpha does not diagnose hEDS. Formal diagnosis sits with a GP, rheumatologist, geneticist or specialist clinic. Alpha assesses, manages and helps you get to the right person. **This must be unambiguous.**
- External link: The Ehlers-Danlos Society

### 5. The Beighton Score, explained properly
**Component:** dedicated content block with anchor (`#beighton-score`)
**Direction:** `beighton score` is 2,400/mo at zero competition and the PAA data confirms it as the top follow-on query. **Own it.** List all nine points; a diagram would be a genuine asset.
**Key points:**
- What it is: a 9-point screening tool
- The nine points, listed: little fingers past 90° (2), thumbs to forearm (2), elbows hyperextending past 10° (2), knees hyperextending past 10° (2), forward flexion with palms flat on the floor (1)
- What the score means — 5 or more commonly indicates generalised hypermobility, with age, sex and ethnicity affecting the threshold
- **The three caveats nobody explains, and this is where the page earns its authority:**
  - It is a *screen*, not a diagnosis
  - It can be negative in someone who is genuinely symptomatic — particularly if you are older, or have had surgery, or your affected joints aren't on the list. **A low score does not mean you're fine.** This is the sentence that will be quoted, shared, and cited by AI answers.
  - It says nothing about symptoms, function or how much pain you're in
- Frame gently as something to bring to an appointment, not to self-diagnose from

### 7. What hypermobility can look like day to day
**Component:** structured list
**Direction:** Comprehensive and recognition-driven. She should find herself here repeatedly. Frame as commonly reported, never as a diagnostic checklist.
**Key points:**
- **Musculoskeletal:** joint pain (shoulders, knees, hips, wrists, jaw); clicking and popping; subluxations and dislocations; soft-tissue injuries that happen too easily; a sense of instability; pain that doesn't match any incident
- **Fatigue** — the most under-recognised symptom. Explain the mechanism. She has never had it explained.
- **Proprioception** — not knowing where your limbs are; clumsiness; rolling ankles; poor balance with eyes closed. Rarely explained anywhere and immediately recognisable.
- **Systemic associations, framed carefully as commonly co-occurring, not caused by and not treated here:** dysautonomia and POTS, mast cell activation, gastrointestinal issues, migraines, anxiety, endometriosis and PCOS, delayed or poor response to local anaesthetic, easy bruising, slow healing
- **Neurodivergence** — `hypermobility and autism` is a confirmed PAA query. Handle it accurately and briefly: an association is reported in the literature, the relationship is not well understood, and Alpha's role is to manage the musculoskeletal side while working alongside whoever else is involved. **Do not overclaim, do not speculate, do not ignore it** — ignoring it leaves the query to worse sources.
- Explicit boundary line closing the section: many of these are outside what an osteopath, physio or exercise physiologist treats. We work with your GP and specialists; we don't replace them.

### 8. How we approach it
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** Where the page converts. Concrete, specific, and clearly different from generic physio. **This section must make the "we've done this before" claim unmistakable.**
**Key points:**
- **A first appointment that is long enough for the whole story** — not just today's sore joint. Most of this reader's frustration is with 15-minute appointments.
- **Strengthening over stretching.** The single clearest positioning statement available. She has too much range already; what she lacks is control. Say it directly — it will be the line she repeats to her friends.
- **Proprioception and control work** — retraining where the joint is in space, not just how strong it is
- **Graded load, and pacing.** Boom-and-bust is the pattern; flare management and pacing is the answer. Name flares explicitly — they are her lived reality and almost no clinic page mentions them.
- **Very gradual progression, and an honest word about what that means:** progress here is measured over months. This is not a six-session plan. Say so upfront — it is honest, it filters for the right patient, and it matches Alpha's stated model of patient commitment.
- **Manual therapy has a role, with caveats** — gentle, judicious, not aggressive end-range work, not manipulation into hypermobile ranges. Being explicit about what Alpha *won't* do is a powerful trust signal for someone who has been hurt by treatment before.
- **Genuinely multidisciplinary:** osteopathy, physiotherapy, exercise physiology, dietetics under one roof, and — per the playbook — actually talking to each other. Fragmented care is this population's core problem, so integration is not a feature here, it is the treatment.
- **Fatigue and energy management** as part of the plan, not an afterthought
- **Online consults** — genuinely important for a population with fatigue, POTS and travel difficulty. Link `/services/online-consults/` and give it real prominence.
- **Working with your other clinicians** — GP, rheumatologist, geneticist, cardiologist, psychologist

## AHPRA Compliance Notes

- **No cure language, ever.** Hypermobility is managed, not cured. The FAQ says so explicitly and the body copy must never drift.
- **Diagnostic scope is the primary risk.** Alpha does not diagnose hEDS or any connective tissue disorder. Every reference to assessment must be paired with the referral pathway. Do not use "diagnose", "confirm", or "test for".
- **Do not use "specialist"** in the AHPRA-regulated sense. "A team with a particular interest and experience in hypermobility" is safe; "hypermobility specialists" is not — even though `hypermobility specialist` is a keyword. Target it in body copy phrasing only, never as a self-description.
- **Comorbidity content (§7) is the second risk.** POTS, MCAS, dysautonomia and gastrointestinal conditions must be described as commonly co-occurring and explicitly outside Alpha's treatment scope. Do not imply treatment helps them.
- **The autism/ADHD content must be conservative** — reported association, mechanism not established, not something Alpha assesses or treats.
- **Ashton's story is a practitioner account, not a testimonial.** It must not be framed as evidence of effectiveness or paired with any outcome claim. Label it clearly.
- Patient testimonials on this page are high-risk given the chronicity of the condition — "I'm pain free" is prohibited. Experience-of-care only.
- No comparative claims about other clinics missing the diagnosis, however tempting. **Describe the pattern, never the practitioners.** This is the page where the trash-talk rule is hardest to hold and most important.
- Named clinician sign-off on the whole page.
