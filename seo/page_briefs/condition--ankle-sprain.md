## Purpose

Take the treatment half of a 7,300/mo cluster, give acute searchers usable first-48-hours guidance with proper red flags, and — the commercial core — convert the person on their third or fourth sprain who has never actually rehabbed one.

## Reader

1. **Acute, hours to days.** Rolled it playing netball, basketball, footy, or on a gutter. Swollen, bruised, limping. Wants to know if it's broken and what to do tonight.
2. **Two to six weeks.** It's better but not right. Still puffy by evening, still can't run properly, still doesn't trust it.
3. **The repeater.** Has done this ankle four times. Tapes it every game. Assumes it's just a dodgy ankle now. This reader has the highest lifetime value and the strongest reason to book, and almost no ankle page on the internet speaks to them.

Five-second need: *is it broken, and what do I do right now.*

### 3. Do you need an X-ray?
**Component:** alert / callout block
**Direction:** High up, because it's the actual question. Give a usable decision rule and a clear instruction, framed as "get it checked", never as a remote clearance.
**Key points:**
- Get it assessed today — GP, urgent care or emergency — if you can't take four steps on it, if there's bone tenderness on the back edge or tip of either ankle bone, if there's obvious deformity, or if the foot is numb, pale or cold
- Say plainly this is a guide used to decide whether imaging is needed, and that the decision belongs to a clinician who examines you
- Note that a bad sprain can hurt as much as a fracture, so pain level alone doesn't tell you
- Also flag: pain on the outside of the foot near the midfoot, or high up between shin and calf (a high ankle sprain) — both are commonly missed and worth mentioning by name

### 4. The first 48 hours
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** The utility block. **Salvage `/education_hub/rolled-ankle/` here** — it exists precisely for this and 301s into this page.
**Key points:**
- Protect and offload early, elevate, compress. Ice for comfort, described honestly.
- Start gentle pain-free movement earlier than most people expect — the ankle-alphabet type movement, weight-bearing as tolerated
- Explicitly counter total rest and prolonged immobilisation: outside of fracture or severe instability, early controlled movement is what current guidance supports
- Crutches or a boot are sometimes appropriate short-term, and a doctor or physio makes that call
- What not to do: aggressive stretching, heat, massaging the acute swelling, testing it by trying to jog on it

### 5. Why "it just got better on its own" is the problem
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** **The page's argument and the reason reader 3 books.** Most people stop at pain-free and never regain what they lost. Make this concrete and slightly uncomfortable.
**Key points:**
- Pain settles well before the ankle recovers — swelling goes, walking is normal, so it's declared fixed
- What's still missing: strength, single-leg balance and the joint's position sense (proprioception), which takes an actual hit and does not return by itself
- Consequence: a meaningfully high proportion of people who sprain an ankle go on to have ongoing symptoms or recurrent sprains. Frame this as a well-recognised pattern rather than quoting a precise statistic unless it can be attributed.
- Chronic ankle instability named and described — the ankle that "gives way" on uneven ground, the constant taping, the reluctance to trust it
- The turn: it isn't a dodgy ankle, it's an unrehabbed one — and that's a different problem with a different answer
- This is the section to give the most words to. It is where the page's commercial value lives.

### 6. How we rehab an ankle properly
**Component:** `approach-steps`
**Direction:** Show the depth. Stages, with what each one is actually for.
**Key points:**
- Assess the joint, the ligaments, the calf complex, and — critically — the hip and foot above and below
- Hands-on work to restore ankle joint range, particularly the dorsiflexion that's almost always lost and almost always missed
- Calf and peroneal strength, built to the loads your sport actually asks for
- Balance and proprioception retraining, progressed properly rather than "stand on one leg while brushing your teeth"
- Hopping, landing and change-of-direction work before return to sport
- Criteria for returning: comparable single-leg strength and hop distance side to side, confidence on uneven ground — not a date
- Honest note on bracing and taping: useful during return, not a permanent solution

### 7. High ankle sprains and other things it might be
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** Short differentiator block. Signals depth and catches long-tail queries.
**Key points:**
- High ankle sprain (syndesmosis) — mechanism, why it hurts higher up, why it takes considerably longer, why misdiagnosis is common
- Medial (inside) sprains — less common, often slower
- Associated injuries worth knowing about: peroneal tendon, small avulsion fractures, cartilage
- Framing: if it isn't tracking as expected by six weeks, it should be reassessed
