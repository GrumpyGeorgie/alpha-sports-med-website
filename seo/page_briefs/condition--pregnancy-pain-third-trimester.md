## Purpose

Serve weeks 28 to birth — the window with the most pain, the least sleep and the shortest patience — with practical, stage-specific help, and be honest that the goal here is getting through the remaining weeks more comfortably, not fixing anything.

## Reader

Weeks 28–40+. She is uncomfortable most of the time, has not slept properly in a month, and is doing arithmetic about how many weeks are left. She has usually already tried the pillow, the belt, the stretches from Instagram, and a bath.

Two things define her differently from the other trimesters:

1. **She has a deadline.** Everything she reads, she reads through "will this help before the baby comes". Slow-burn rehab framing lands badly. Be concrete about what changes this week.
2. **She has often been told there's no point starting now.** That is the version of "not being listened to" that shows up in this trimester, and it is the emotional hook for the page.

A significant subgroup has pelvic girdle pain severe enough to affect walking, stairs and getting dressed — and is quietly worried about how she'll manage a newborn.

**First five seconds must deliver:** yes, there is still a point to doing something at 34 weeks; here is what actually helps in the last stretch; and here is exactly when to stop reading and ring your maternity team.

## Search Intent

Informational, with the highest booking conversion of the three trimester pages — the pain is at its worst and the tolerance for enduring it is at its lowest. **`pubic symphysis pain pregnancy` (260/mo, competition 0.01) peaks in this window**, and while the hub owns that term, this page should be the one that deep-links from it with the severe-presentation content.

The distinctive term opportunity here is **carpal tunnel in pregnancy** — a genuinely third-trimester presentation, commonly missed, and almost never covered by clinic sites.

## What makes this page genuinely different

**Nothing below belongs on the hub or the other two trimester pages:**

1. **Carpal tunnel syndrome and hand/wrist pain from fluid retention.** Third trimester, mechanism entirely different from anything else in the cluster, links out to `/conditions/hand-wrist-pain/`, and almost nobody connects it to pregnancy. The page's most valuable original content.
2. **Sleep is the dominant problem.** On the earlier pages it is a footnote. Here it is a section — positioning, pillows, the left-side advice and what it's actually for, rolling over without pain, and what to do at 3am.
3. **Severe PGP that limits weight-bearing.** Different in kind, not degree — this is the presentation where mobility aids, workplace modification and genuine functional planning come in.
4. **Positioning constraints are at maximum.** Treatment is side-lying only; supine is generally avoided; sessions are shorter; getting on and off the table is itself a consideration. Concrete and reassuring to describe.
5. **Preparing for the fourth trimester** — practical planning for feeding positions, lifting a capsule, prams, and the reality of a newborn with a painful pelvis. Forward-looking content that exists nowhere else in the silo.
6. **The red flags are at their most consequential.** Pre-eclampsia, reduced fetal movements, preterm labour, PPROM. This block is the most important on the page.
7. **"Is it too late to start?"** — a stage-specific objection the other pages don't have.

### 2. The last stretch
**Component:** `problem-statement`
**Direction:** ~130 words. Recognition first. This reader is worn down and the tone should meet her there without pity.
**Key points:**
- Name the actual daily experience: rolling over in bed, getting off the couch, the walk from the car park, standing at the kitchen bench, being awake at 3am
- Explain why it peaks now: maximum load, maximum ligament laxity, the abdominal wall doing the least stabilising work it will do, fluid retention, and no sleep to recover with
- Address the objection head-on: **"there's no point starting now" is the most common thing people are told at 32 weeks, and it isn't true.** Positioning changes, load management and targeted hands-on work can change how the next six weeks feel. Frame as *can*, not *will*.
- Do not promise the pain will go.

### 3. Pelvic girdle and pubic symphysis pain at its worst
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** The hub explains what PGP and SPD are. **This page covers the severe end** — the version that affects walking and function. Deep-link up to the hub anchor for the explanation and spend the words here on what to actually do.
**Key points:**
- What severe looks like: pain rolling over, difficulty on stairs, pain on single-leg tasks, a limp, audible clicking or grinding, waking every time you move
- The practical rules that make the biggest difference: keep the legs together, sit to dress, swing both legs together in and out of the car and bed, satin sheet or plastic bag under the hips to reduce friction rolling over, pillow between the knees, stairs one at a time, shorter more frequent walks rather than one long one
- Support belts and taping — worth a proper trial, fitted rather than guessed
- Crutches or a wheelchair for short periods are not a failure. If mobility is significantly affected, say so to your maternity team.
- Workplace: what to ask for and why it is reasonable to ask
- Honest framing: **most PGP improves substantially after birth.** Say so — it is the single most reassuring true thing available. And note that where it doesn't, that is treatable too → /conditions/postpartum-pain/

### 4. Hand and wrist pain, and pins and needles at night
**Component:** dedicated content block with its own H2 and anchor (`#carpal-tunnel`)
**Direction:** **The page's original-content flagship.** Write it as a standalone answer block — most readers have no idea this is a pregnancy thing.
**Key points:**
- Clean definition paragraph (40–55 words) suitable for a snippet: fluid retention in the third trimester increases pressure in the carpal tunnel, compressing the median nerve
- What it feels like: numbness, tingling or burning in the thumb, index and middle fingers; worst overnight and first thing in the morning; shaking the hand out for relief; dropping things
- Why it happens now and not earlier
- What helps: night splints (specifically — this is the most effective and most under-used intervention), positioning, activity modification, hands-on work through the wrist, forearm and neck, and checking the neck as a contributor
- **It usually resolves in the weeks to months after birth.** Say so.
- **Escalate:** constant numbness, weakness, or wasting of the thumb muscles needs medical review. Sudden or severe swelling of the hands and face is a pre-eclampsia flag — see the red-flag block.
- Link: /conditions/hand-wrist-pain/

### 5. Sleep, and how to survive it
**Component:** standard content
**Direction:** The most-read section on the page. Practical, specific, no filler.
**Key points:**
- Side-lying with a pillow between the knees and one supporting the bump; pillow behind the back to stop rolling
- The left-side advice: explain what it's actually about (blood flow via the vena cava) and be reasonable about it — the guidance is to settle to sleep on your side, and waking up on your back is not a catastrophe. **Confirm current wording with a clinician.**
- Getting out of bed without provoking the pelvis: roll as one unit, legs together, push up from side-lying
- Hip pain from side-lying on a firm mattress — a mattress topper is a real intervention
- Heartburn, reflux and needing to prop up — where it interacts with rib and upper back pain
- Honest: sleep in the third trimester is genuinely hard. We can improve the mechanical part of it. We can't fix all of it.

### 6. Movement, walking and training after 28 weeks
**Component:** short content block
**Key points:**
- Something most days beats nothing, but the shape of it changes — shorter, more frequent, lower impact
- Swimming and water-based movement is often the best tolerated when the pelvis is painful. Worth naming specifically.
- Most running has stopped by now for most people, and that is not a failure — link forward to how return to running works after birth
- Strength work continues in modified positions; standing and side-lying variations
- Pelvic floor: keep it in the picture, but scope carefully (see Open Questions on internal assessment)
- Parameters set with the maternity team
- Link: /services/exercise-physiology/, /services/clinical-pilates/, /conditions/running-injuries/

### 7. When to contact your maternity team — now
**Component:** callout / alert block, visually distinct
**Direction:** **The most important block on the page.** Third-trimester red flags are the highest-consequence in the cluster. Clinician sign-off mandatory. Do not soften, do not abbreviate, do not bury.
**Key points:**
- **Reduced or changed fetal movements — contact your maternity assessment unit immediately. Do not wait until morning. Do not book an appointment with us first.** This must be the first item and visually the most prominent.
- Severe or persistent headache, visual disturbance or flashing lights, sudden swelling of face, hands or feet, pain under the ribs on the right, or feeling generally very unwell — possible pre-eclampsia, urgent
- Vaginal bleeding
- Fluid loss / waters breaking
- Regular tightenings or contractions before 37 weeks
- Fever, or pain on passing urine
- Calf pain or swelling, chest pain, sudden breathlessness — emergency
- New numbness or weakness in the legs, saddle numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control — emergency, any stage
- Close with the maternity assessment unit / Nurse-on-Call pathway. **Confirm exact Victorian wording.**

### 8. What an appointment looks like at 34 weeks
**Component:** `approach-steps` (abbreviated)

## AHPRA Compliance Notes

- Inherits every guardrail from the pregnancy hub brief. Read it first.
- **Highest-risk section on the page is 9.** Any drift from ergonomics into birth preparation, "getting the body ready for labour", pelvic "opening", or fetal positioning is prohibited. Keep it to feeding set-up, lifting and prams.
- Do not claim treatment shortens labour, eases birth, or speeds recovery.
