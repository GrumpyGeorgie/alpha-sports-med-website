## 3. What myotherapy actually is

**H2:** What is myotherapy?

**Concept callout:** Assessment first. We look at why the tissue is behaving this way, not only where it hurts.

**Copy:**
Myotherapy is the assessment and treatment of musculoskeletal pain and dysfunction that comes from muscles and soft tissue: the muscles, fascia, tendons and connective tissue that let you move.

Myotherapists train through an advanced diploma of myotherapy or a bachelor of health science in clinical myotherapy. Unlike our osteopaths, physiotherapists and chiropractors, myotherapists aren't registered through AHPRA. The profession is self-regulated through professional associations, and we'd rather say that plainly. See how each of our disciplines is qualified on our qualifications page.

A session may include soft tissue and deep tissue techniques, trigger point therapy, myofascial techniques, dry needling, joint mobilisation, stretching, taping and home advice. When people talk about "knots", they usually mean trigger points: small, tender, tight spots within a muscle that can feel sore when pressed and sometimes send discomfort elsewhere.

What sets myotherapy apart is the assessment. Your myotherapist tests how you move, checks range of motion and looks at the tissue involved, then builds a working explanation of why it's behaving the way it is.

## 4. Myotherapy vs remedial massage

**H3 (inside the explainer):** Myotherapy or remedial massage?

**Copy:**
Both are hands-on soft tissue treatments, and both can help a tight back feel better.

- **Remedial massage:** therapists typically train to diploma level and focus on treating the tissue presented.
- **Myotherapy:** longer training, a broader set of techniques including dry needling, and an assessment step aimed at the source of the problem as well as its site.

If you want a good treatment for a tight back, either can help. If you want someone to work out why the same back keeps tightening, myotherapy is set up for that.

## 5. Dry needling

**H3 (inside the explainer):** What about dry needling?

**Copy:**
Dry needling places fine, single-use filament needles into trigger points within a muscle. It uses the same kind of needle as acupuncture but different reasoning: dry needling is based on a Western musculoskeletal framework, while acupuncture comes from traditional Chinese medicine.

Most people feel a brief twitch or a deep ache, and next-day soreness similar to a hard training session is common. Bruising can occur, and like any needle-based technique there are less common risks, which your myotherapist will explain first. It's always optional, and you can have a full treatment without it.

## 8. Your first appointment

**H2:** Your first myotherapy appointment

**Intro:** A clear, conversational process with no surprises.

1. **Tell us what's going on.** We ask about your symptoms, your history, your work and training, and what you want to get back to.
2. **Move through an assessment.** Your myotherapist checks how you move and the tissue involved, and explains what they notice.
3. **Agree on treatment.** Options, including whether dry needling is suitable for you, are explained before hands-on care begins.

**You set the pressure:** Firm doesn't have to mean painful. Tell us what feels okay and we'll adjust.
**Expect some soreness:** Feeling sore for a day or two is common, especially after deep tissue work or dry needling.

## 11. FAQs

**H2:** Myotherapy FAQs

**What's the difference between myotherapy and remedial massage?**
Both are hands-on soft tissue treatments. Myotherapists complete longer training, use a broader range of techniques including dry needling, and assess where a problem may be coming from as well as where you feel it. Remedial massage is a good option with a different scope.

**Can I claim myotherapy on private health?**
You may be able to. Cover for myotherapy depends on your fund, your level of extras cover and the myotherapist's professional association membership. Check with your fund before your appointment.

**Do I need a referral?**
No. You can book a myotherapist directly without a GP referral.

**Does it hurt, and will I be sore afterwards?**
Some techniques feel firm or tender, especially on tight areas, and we'll work within what's comfortable for you. Soreness for a day or two afterwards is common, a bit like after a hard training session. If anything feels unusual or doesn't settle, let us know or speak with your GP.

**What is dry needling, and do I have to have it?**
Dry needling places fine, single-use needles into trigger points within a muscle. It can cause a twitch, a deep ache and next-day soreness, and bruising is possible. It's always optional: your myotherapist will explain it, check it's suitable for you and only go ahead with your consent.

**How often should I come?**
It depends on what's going on and what you want. Some people book regular maintenance sessions; others want to understand why a problem keeps returning. Your myotherapist will suggest a plan after your assessment and review it with you.

**Should I book myotherapy, or see an osteo or physio first?**
If your main concern is tight, sore muscles, myotherapy is a reasonable place to start. If you've had a recent injury, or your pain is sharp, worsening or spreading, an osteopath or physiotherapist may be a better first step. If you're not sure, tell us what's going on and we'll point you to the right person. For new or unexplained symptoms, your GP is always a good place to start.

**What should I wear?**
Comfortable clothing you can move in, such as activewear. We may need access to the area being treated, and we'll always explain what we're doing and check you're comfortable first.
